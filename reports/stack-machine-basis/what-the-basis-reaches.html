What the Basis Reaches

The 18-op guarded stack machine, measured against wl-core-v1 — the 118-function basis it was proposed to replace. Reachability, the semantic monoid, per-op ablation, and what a single step costs in bits.

The short version

  • 01The new basis is cheaper per step, not more expressive per step. Both bases generate new behaviour at almost exactly the same rate under composition — 5.87× per op against 5.77×. The stack machine simply pays less to do it.
  • 02The efficiency gain is real and it is about 25%. Per step you spend 3.48 bits and gain 2.55 bits of new behaviour, against 4.29 spent for 2.53 gained on the old basis. 73% efficient versus 59%.
  • 03The issue's central claim about the old basis does not survive contact with the measurement. wl-core-v1 does not have a near-trivial composition monoid. It grows steadily and shows no sign of saturating.
  • 04Most of the new basis's reachable set is inert. 64% of the states at depth 8 are literal stacking — constants pushed and never used. That fraction is falling, but slowly.
  • 05No op is dead weight, but you would not know it from reachability. Six ops look worthless by reachable-state count and are worth 4.6–8.8% each by behaviour.
  • 06Against that, programs are twice as long. Median 2.00× the ops for the same integer target across 41 targets, measured against an exhaustive expansion of the old basis. Every bias in that number makes it a conservative estimate.
01

What one step costs

The unit of search is a step. Its price is the entropy of the choice you make: how many ops could go here, in this state. Guards are what make that number smaller than the basis.

Stack machine11.24 of 18 ops legal, on average. Guards remove 6.76.
wl-core-v120.52 of 118 functions applicable. Types remove 97.5.
Bits per step3.48 down from log₂18 = 4.17
Bits per step4.29 down from log₂118 = 6.88

The guard saves 0.69 bits. Type masking on the old basis saves 2.59. That looks like a rout until you notice where the two land: 3.48 bits against 4.29. The old basis has more to throw away because it started with more. What matters for search is the number you end up paying, and there the stack machine is 0.81 bits — about 43% — cheaper.

Both numbers overstate the real branching, because different ops can land on the same state. Counting distinct successors instead: 8.83 for the stack machine, 13.08 for the old basis. Roughly a third of each basis's apparent choice is duplicate work.

That 11.24 is not an artifact of starting from an empty stack. Run the same search from four very different roots and it barely moves — even though the roots themselves offer anywhere from 5 to 15 legal ops, their neighbourhoods all converge on the same number within a step or two. The guard's effect is a property of the machine, not of where you start, which makes b ≈ 11.3 a constant you can put in a search-cost model.

02

What composition generates

Reachability from one root answers what states exist. That is the wrong question for expressiveness, and on this basis it is actively misleading — it scores a contracting op like eq? at zero, because booleans were already reachable by pushing true.

The right question is how many distinct behaviours the ops generate when composed. Fix a set of probe inputs; a program's behaviour is the tuple of results it gives on every probe, with a hole where its guard refuses. Two programs are the same element of the monoid when those tuples agree. BFS over behaviours enumerates the monoid, and its growth rate compares directly across bases of different sizes. Eight probes each.

This contradicts the premise of #42. The issue argues the old basis is “a near-trivial composition monoid” — that half its functions are idempotent or involutive, so it “generates almost nothing under composition.” On this measurement that is false. wl-core-v1 generates new behaviour at 5.77× per step and is still going at length 6.

What the issue's actual evidence shows — BFS solving random_inputs_500_shortest_v1 100% at depth 4 — is that the corpus is trivial, not that the basis is. The corpus was generated from that basis, so its reachability was never in question. Those are different claims, and only the weaker one is supported.

Where the stack machine does win is price. Dividing behaviour gained by bits spent:

Stack machine73% 2.55 bits of new behaviour per 3.48 bits of choice
wl-core-v159% 2.53 bits of new behaviour per 4.29 bits of choice

That 25% relative gain is the honest headline. It is a real improvement in search economics and it is much smaller than the framing in #42 implies.

03

Most of the space is inert

Five of the eighteen ops are literals, and a literal is always legal. That gives the machine a free five-way branch in every state, and most of what BFS finds is the consequence: stacks of constants that no later op ever touches.

The same fact shows up in the search tree: 91% of all states are first discovered by a literal push, and only 9% by an op that computes something. And it shows up in the redundancy — at length 7 there are 4,724,725 executable programs mapping onto 138,725 states, 34 programs per state, rising by roughly 2× per level.

This is the most actionable finding in the report. Literals are a fifth of the basis and generate the majority of the search space's dead weight.

04

What each op is worth

Drop one op, rebuild the monoid, and see how much behaviour disappears. This is the measurement #41 needs in order to decide what a basis should contain.

Measured by reachability instead, six ops look worthless. div, eq?, lt?, null?, drop and mul each add 0.0% to the reachable-state count at depth 7 — they compute new values but land on states that were already reachable another way. By behaviour they are worth 4.6–8.8% each.

If you rank a basis by reachability you will delete exactly the ops that do arithmetic and comparison. Use the monoid.

05

The shape of the graph

Reversible edges34% of transitions have an inverse transition — swap swap, dup drop. Pure search waste.
Growth per depth5.5× reachable states, stable from depth 3 to depth 9
Biggest attractor117 distinct ways to reach the stack [ false ] within depth 5

That last number is eq? and lt? doing their job. Every pair of unequal values on the stack collapses to the same [ false ]. Predicates are contractions: enormous in-degree, and they destroy the information that distinguishes their inputs. Good for finding a program, bad for telling states apart.

Values, by contrast, grow fast. The largest integer reachable at depth d follows 22(d−3)/2 — doubly exponential, by repeated dup mul: 2 at depth 3, 16 at depth 7, 256 at depth 9.

But the old basis climbs faster. It has square and double as single functions, where the stack machine needs dup mul — two ops per squaring, and two more to build the 2 in the first place.

That is one frontier. Widening it to every integer both bases can build — 41 targets, with the old basis expanded exhaustively, so its figures are exact shortest paths rather than upper bounds — gives the same answer with a distribution behind it.

Three separate biases in this number all push the same way. The stack machine's costs are shortest-within-four-stack-cells, so they are upper bounds. Targets the old basis cannot reach within six ops are excluded, which drops exactly the cases where it does worst. And five of the targets use the shortcut below. Correct for any of them and the ratio falls, never rises: 2× is the conservative end.

Where a big heterogeneous basis gets some of its speed. wl-core-v1 reaches 63 in three ops as int_to_str → str_hash → int_bit_length, and 100 as int_to_str → str_hash → int_clip_0_100. It is not computing 63; it is reading the bit length off a SHA-256 digest. str_hash is a deterministic pseudorandom constant source, and bit_length, popcount, clip and length are cheap projections off it.

It is legitimate — the paths are reproducible and genuinely shortest — but it is a lookup table, not compositional structure. Removing str_hash lengthens 5 of 46 targets and moves the mean from 2.07× to 1.97×. Small, and worth knowing about before reading any corpus generated from that basis as evidence of reasoning depth.

So: the d-inflation #42 predicted is real and it is worth about 2×. Set against a 25% per-step efficiency gain, arithmetic comes out behind. Whether the trade nets out over real tasks needs a semantically matched corpus, which does not exist yet — that is what the issue's “done when” actually calls for, and it is the one thing still missing.

06

What I would do next

  • Do not delete ops on reachability evidence. The monoid and the reachable set disagree about six of eighteen ops, and the monoid is the one that tracks what programs can compute.
  • Look hard at the literals. They are the single largest source of inert branching. Making a literal cost more fuel than an op, or gating pushes on stack depth, would change the search economics more than any other single change measured here.
  • Retire the “trivial monoid” argument for #42. The substrate still has three good reasons — one structural notion of identity, guards as an exact first-class mask, and abstraction by naming a substring. Expressiveness is not one of them, and the issue is stronger without a claim that measurement contradicts.
  • The kill criterion is still open. #42 says to reconsider if certified generation at distance 4 is infeasible. It is comfortably feasible — depth 4 is 865 states from empty, and depth 9 is 4.2M. Certification is not the constraint; d-inflation is.

What would change these numbers. The monoid is measured on eight probes per basis, so every count is a lower bound — more probes distinguish more behaviours. The two probe sets are different types of object, so the comparison is close but not exact. The stack machine's count is flattered by literals, which manufacture new behaviour from nothing; the old basis cannot do that. And wl-core-v1 was only taken to length 6 against the stack machine's 8.
